No. 02 Brand Refresh

When should a company rebrand?

[Author name] · [Publish date] · [Reading time]

02 The Grandyra Journal

Issue 02 of the Grandyra Journal.

Rebranding is one of the most expensive decisions a company can make, and one of the easiest to make for the wrong reasons. A new CEO wants a fresh start. Someone saw a competitor's new logo. The office is being renovated anyway.

None of those are good reasons on their own. Here's how we think about it.

Good reasons to rebrand

  • The business has changed. You started as a trading company and now you manufacture. You sold one product; now you sell twenty. The brand still describes the old company.
  • You're entering a new market. A brand that works in Dhaka may not travel to Dubai or to a younger audience at home.
  • A merger or acquisition. Two brands, one company — something has to give.
  • Your reputation is holding you back. Rarely solved by design alone, but a rebrand can mark a genuine change in behaviour.
  • Nobody can use the brand properly. If every new brochure needs an exception to the guidelines, the system is broken.

Reasons that usually aren't enough

  • The team is bored of the logo. (Customers are usually not.)
  • A competitor rebranded.
  • It's been a while.
Your team sees the brand every day. Your customers see it for a few seconds. Fatigue is felt inside long before it's felt outside.

Refresh or rebrand?

Most companies don't need a revolution. A brand refresh keeps what people already recognise — a colour, a symbol, a name — and fixes what's holding the brand back: dated typography, a messy system, an unclear message.

A full rebrand makes sense when the old brand is actively working against where the business is going.

How to decide

Start with an honest audit. Gather everything the brand touches, talk to customers and staff, and ask one question: does this still describe who we are and where we're going? The answer usually makes the scale of the change obvious.
